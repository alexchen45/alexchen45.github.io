A learning guide for UX designers

Applying Psychology to B2B UX and Agent-Building Systems

A practical way to move from vague product briefs to human-centered design by studying work, cognitive friction, coordination, expertise, automation, and evidence.

Academic grounding Industry translation Worked agent example Interactive exercises
Orientation

How to use this guide

Read it linearly once. Later, return by the kind of friction you are seeing rather than by theory name.

This guide begins with the high-level problem: why psychology feels easy to apply to behavior-change products but difficult to apply to a brief such as “design a B2B SaaS platform.” It then gives you a repeatable method, a theory map organized around practical bottlenecks, a deep treatment of agent-building systems, and a way to work responsibly when direct user access is limited.

Each theory section separates academic grounding from industry application. The first tells you what the theory actually explains. The second shows what it can change in a product decision. The distinction matters because a theory is useful only when it explains a mechanism and leads to a testable design hypothesis.

The central idea Do not begin with a psychology principle and search for somewhere to apply it. Begin with the work, locate the human friction, then choose a theory that explains why the friction occurs.
Part I

Reframing the problem

Chapter 1

The human center of enterprise design is the work system

“Design a B2B SaaS system” is not a human goal. It is a proposed class of solution.

Psychology feels straightforward when the behavioral outcome is already defined. “Help someone maintain a healthy diet” gives you an actor, a desired behavior, and a family of theories related to motivation, habits, self-regulation, and social influence.

Enterprise briefs usually hide those ingredients. The software may be mandatory. A manager may assign the goal. The purchaser may not use the product. Several people may contribute to one outcome. Success may be measured by compliance, throughput, risk, cost, or service quality rather than by personal satisfaction.

The useful question is therefore not merely, “What does this user want?” It is:

What human work will this system change, and what psychological demands does that work place on people?

This remains human-centered. ISO 9241-210 treats human-centered design as a lifecycle process concerned with users, interactive systems, and their context of use, not as a requirement that every product begin with a voluntary personal aspiration.[1]

Why the unit of analysis must expand

In B2B design, the person clicking the button is often only one part of the system. The work may involve a daily operator, approver, administrator, subject matter expert, auditor, manager, customer, and a downstream team. Their incentives and risks differ.

A narrow framing

Can the support agent complete the refund form efficiently?

A work-system framing

Can the organization resolve an unusual refund correctly, preserve the reason for the decision, hand the case across teams, and show later who approved it?

Human-centered design is not the opposite of goal-centered design. It asks broader questions:

  • Whose goal is being optimized?
  • Who performs, approves, supports, and experiences the consequences of the work?
  • What knowledge and judgment are required?
  • What happens when the normal process fails?
  • What power does the product give, remove, or redistribute?
  • How will we know whether the work improved rather than merely whether feature usage increased?
Important distinction: A business outcome such as “reduce case-handling time by 30%” is not yet a user need. The human needs underneath it may include recognizing priority, resuming interrupted work, avoiding re-entry, finding relevant policy, and understanding what another team already did.

1. Describe the work

Start with the real object being transformed and the outcome that must become true. “Configure an agent” is an interface action. “Create a support agent that answers according to policy, escalates risky cases, and can be maintained by the support team” is a work outcome.

Map six things:

Actors

Who performs, approves, administers, reviews, buys, supports, and receives the output?

Objects

What is being transformed: a case, decision, model, contract, workflow, account, or deployment?

Constraints

Permissions, regulation, legacy systems, deadlines, incomplete data, incentives, and dependencies.

Decisions

Where is judgment required, what cues matter, and what makes one decision better than another?

Coordination

What must survive handoffs? Who owns the next step? Who needs awareness without another meeting?

Exceptions

Missing information, failed integrations, conflicting policy, unavailable approvers, or unusual cases.

2. Name the human friction

Most enterprise problems can be classified as one or more of the following. The classification is not the answer. It narrows the kind of explanation you need.

FrictionDiagnostic question
PerceptionCan people notice the important state, cue, or change?
ComprehensionCan they understand what the information means in the domain?
ProjectionCan they anticipate what will happen next?
MemoryMust they remember information the system could preserve or expose?
DecisionCan they judge tradeoffs, uncertainty, and consequences?
ActionCan they express intent precisely and efficiently?
LearningCan novices form a useful model and experts remain efficient?
CoordinationCan people understand ownership, history, and others’ activity?
TrustDo they rely on automation when, and only when, they should?
ControlCan they intervene, correct, pause, undo, or override?
RecoveryCan they diagnose and repair failures without losing work?
AdoptionDoes use fit incentives, social expectations, support, and job performance?

3. Select a mechanism, not a slogan

A theory should explain why the friction occurs. “Cognitive load” is not useful unless you can identify which interacting elements must be held together and which burden is caused by the presentation. “Trust” is not useful unless you can describe the evidence people use to decide whether to rely on the system.

4. Write a falsifiable design hypothesis

Weak rationale:

We should make the agent more transparent.

Testable rationale:

Because builders cannot see which instruction, source, or tool produced an answer, they cannot diagnose failures. A trace that connects output to instructions, retrieved sources, and tool actions should reduce diagnosis time and improve successful correction.

5. Decide what evidence could change your mind

Measure the work outcome and the predicted mechanism. In the example above, measure time to locate the earliest divergence, successful correction, and whether people interpret the trace accurately. Do not stop at clicks on the trace panel.

Chapter 3

Making an ambiguous brief tractable

When goals are unclear, model the work domain and its constraints before you model the interface.

Cognitive Work Analysis was developed for complex sociotechnical systems where work cannot be reduced to a stable sequence of tasks. Its first phase, Work Domain Analysis, describes the functional structure and constraints of the environment rather than starting from a list of user actions.[3]

You do not need to perform a formal Cognitive Work Analysis for every SaaS project. A lighter version is valuable because it forces the team to discuss purpose, tradeoffs, decisions, and failure before screens.

The five-question work-domain review

  1. What does this system exist to make true in the world?
    Example: customer claims are resolved correctly, consistently, and within the promised time.
  2. What must stay in balance?
    Speed versus accuracy, automation leverage versus retained control, standardization versus exception handling, cost versus risk.
  3. What decisions must humans make?
    List decisions, not screens or tasks. Define what distinguishes a good decision from a bad one.
  4. What must people perceive, know, remember, or retrieve to make those decisions?
  5. What happens when the system or process fails, and who bears the consequence?
The layer teams often skip The balancing constraints usually contain the actual product strategy. A claim system is not merely trying to move cases faster. It is maintaining a balance among throughput, fairness, fraud risk, customer trust, and auditability.

A practical abstraction ladder

LevelQuestionAgent-builder example
PurposeWhy does the system exist?Enable dependable automation of knowledge work.
Values and balancesWhat must be optimized or held in balance?Automation leverage, control, quality, cost, privacy, and accountability.
FunctionsWhat functions maintain those balances?Define, test, review, deploy, monitor, intervene, and improve.
ComponentsWhat capabilities perform the functions?Instructions, knowledge, tools, policies, tests, versions, traces, permissions.
Visible formHow are those capabilities represented?Canvas, structured editor, run log, test suite, deployment panel, review queue.

The lower layers should support the higher ones. If the product claims to balance automation and control but its visible form contains only a prompt box and a Publish button, the interface does not represent the work domain it claims to support.

Part II

The theory map for B2B systems

Chapter 4

Work is a cognitive system

The most useful enterprise theories often treat cognition as distributed across people, tools, representations, and time.

Distributed cognition

When important knowledge lives across people and artifacts
Academic grounding source

Hutchins’ work studies cognition in real settings and treats the functional cognitive system as larger than one mind. Representations, instruments, procedures, roles, and communication can carry part of the reasoning and memory required to perform work.[2]

Industry application

Before redesigning a workflow, find the spreadsheets, notebooks, message threads, saved queries, checklists, and unofficial experts. These may be load-bearing parts of the current cognitive system.

Design shared records, persistent context, decision rationale, provenance, ownership, and links to the places where work already happens. Do not replace a spreadsheet until you understand the organizational memory encoded in it.

Useful design questions
  • Where does knowledge live now?
  • What information disappears at a handoff?
  • What does one role know that another must reconstruct?
  • Which unofficial artifact would break the workflow if removed?

Joint cognitive systems

When the unit of performance is human plus technology
Academic grounding

Cognitive systems engineering examines how people and technology jointly maintain control in complex environments. The question is not whether the interface is independently intelligent or easy. It is whether the combined system can adapt, detect problems, and achieve the outcome.

Industry application

Evaluate the total system. An AI may improve average classification accuracy while making unusual failures harder for people to detect. A workflow may reduce clicks for one role while increasing coordination cost for three others.

Measure combined performance, recovery, and adaptability rather than model performance or individual task time alone.

Useful design questions
  • Which work should the system perform, and which judgment should remain human?
  • Can the person detect when automation leaves its competence region?
  • Does automation preserve or erode the person’s ability to recover?

Situation awareness

When people must perceive, understand, and anticipate system state
Academic grounding source

Endsley describes situation awareness as perception of relevant elements, comprehension of their meaning, and projection of likely future state. Attention and working memory constrain all three, while mental models help experienced operators interpret information.[4]

Industry application

A useful dashboard should answer: What is happening? What is abnormal? Why? What is at risk? What requires action? What will happen if nobody acts? What changed since I last looked?

A large collection of metrics is not situation awareness. Organize displays around decisions, consequences, and changes.

Agent-system implications

Expose live version, current run state, recent changes, tool failures, escalations, cost, cases awaiting review, and the predicted effect of publishing a change.

Workspace awareness

When coordination depends on seeing others’ activity
Academic grounding source

Dourish and Bellotti found that awareness embedded passively in a shared workspace can support coordination without requiring every participant to generate explicit status messages.[10]

Industry application

Support questions such as: Who changed this? Why? Has anyone reviewed it? Which version is under discussion? Is someone else editing it? Who owns the next step?

Useful patterns include activity history, presence, review state, change attribution, comments attached to the work object, handoff summaries, and notifications based on responsibility.

Design caution

The goal is not maximum visibility. Excessive surveillance can harm autonomy and trust. Show activity that helps coordination, and make monitoring boundaries explicit.

Chapter 5

Understanding, learning, and expertise

Enterprise users may be novices in the software and experts in the work. Design must respect both facts.

Mental models

When people cannot predict what the system will do
Academic grounding

People form internal explanations from terminology, visible structure, feedback, documentation, and prior experience. The interface does not need to expose the implementation literally. It must support sufficiently accurate prediction and action.

Industry application

Define a coherent conceptual model before polishing navigation. In an agent product, distinguish agent, instruction, knowledge, tool, policy, test, run, and version. Use the same relationships in authoring, debugging, deployment, and documentation.

Research prompts
  • What do you think will happen next?
  • Where would you expect this setting to apply?
  • How would you explain this system to a colleague?
  • What would you inspect when it behaves incorrectly?

Cognitive load and element interactivity

When many interacting elements must be understood together
Academic grounding source

Cognitive load research emphasizes element interactivity: difficulty increases when several pieces must be processed together because their relationships matter. Some interactivity belongs to the domain. Some is added by fragmented or poorly organized presentation.

Industry application

Identify what people must keep in mind: values across tabs, dependencies between rules, rationale from a previous screen, or the impact of one configuration on another. Bring related information together, support comparison, preserve context, and externalize memory.

Design caution

“Reduce cognitive load” does not mean reduce information density. An expert may prefer a dense table because it keeps a known schema visible and reduces navigation.

Expertise reversal

When novice guidance becomes expert friction
Academic grounding

Knowledge changes how information is chunked. Detailed explanations, worked examples, and stepwise guidance can help novices but become redundant or disruptive for experienced users.

Industry application

For novices, use templates, examples, safe defaults, guided setup, and feedback. For experts, provide bulk actions, keyboard support, reusable components, saved views, APIs, and direct access to advanced configuration.

Guidance should fade. Efficiency should grow with experience.

Evaluation

Recruit by domain expertise and product experience separately. “Expert user” is not one variable.

Self-determination at work

When mandatory tools undermine agency or mastery
Academic grounding source

Workplace applications of Self-Determination Theory focus on autonomy, competence, and relatedness, and on how motivation can become internalized rather than remaining purely controlled by external pressure.[14]

Industry application
  • Autonomy: overrides, escape routes, flexible paths, and control over automation.
  • Competence: feedback that develops skill, visible mastery, and tools that reward growing expertise.
  • Relatedness: shared artifacts, attribution, and collaboration that feels supportive rather than surveillant.
Design caution

Do not use SDT to make workers enjoy an exploitative process. Product design cannot compensate for harmful policy, incentives, or management.

Chapter 6

Expert decisions, searching, and diagnosis

Much of B2B UX is not data entry. It is judgment under time, uncertainty, and incomplete information.

Naturalistic decision making

When experts recognize patterns rather than compare every option
Academic grounding

Recognition-primed decision research describes how experienced practitioners often recognize a situation from salient cues, retrieve a plausible action, and mentally test whether it will work instead of generating a large option set.

Industry application

Do not automatically build a wizard or comparison matrix for expert judgment. First learn which cues allow practitioners to classify a case and which anomalies make them reconsider.

Surface high-value cues, historical analogues, and deviations from the expected pattern. Make anomalies loud without turning every state into an alert.

Best research method

Use incident-centered interviews. Ask an expert to reconstruct a difficult case, then probe cues, expectations, options, uncertainty, and what a novice would have missed.

Information foraging

When users spend more time finding evidence than acting on it
Academic grounding source

Information foraging theory models how people follow cues that suggest where useful information is likely to be found and decide whether to continue searching in one information “patch” or move elsewhere.[9]

Industry application

Improve information scent with domain-specific labels, meaningful previews, match explanations, preserved search context, breadcrumbs, linked evidence, and errors connected to likely causes.

Ask: Where would the person look first, and what cue tells them they are getting warmer?

Agent-system implications

Link outputs to sources, instructions, tool results, and policy. Let users move from symptom to likely cause without manually reconstructing the run across multiple panels.

Progressive evaluation

When users cannot inspect partial work or catch divergence early
Academic grounding

In the Cognitive Dimensions framework, progressive evaluation asks whether partially completed work can be checked. It is especially important for exploratory design and programming-like activities.[5]

Industry application

Provide previews, step-level execution, simulations, sample data, validation, test cases, version comparison, and feedback before consequential commitment.

Measure

Measure how early users detect a wrong assumption, not only whether the final artifact succeeds.

Error taxonomy and recovery

When the system treats every failure as “user error”
Academic grounding

Human-factors work distinguishes slips, lapses, mistakes, and violations. These have different causes and therefore require different design responses.

Industry application

A slip may need undo and safer controls. A mistake may need a better conceptual model. A lapse may need external memory. A deliberate workaround may indicate that the official process cannot handle real work.

Design requirement

Error messages should identify what happened, likely causes, affected work, safe recovery steps, and what will be preserved. “Contact your administrator” is not recovery guidance.

Chapter 7

Coordination, handoffs, and the messy path

The happy path is a plan. Enterprise work is situated, interruptible, and full of exceptions.

Situated action

Plans can orient action, but actual work develops in response to current circumstances, available resources, interruptions, and unexpected events. This perspective is associated with Lucy Suchman’s study of plans and situated action.[15]

A process diagram may say:

  1. Receive request
  2. Review
  3. Approve
  4. Complete

Real work says:

  • The required data is missing.
  • The approver is unavailable.
  • The case belongs to two teams.
  • The customer has a special contract.
  • A policy changed yesterday.
  • The recommendation looks wrong.
  • The person resumes the case two weeks later.
  • Someone completed part of the work outside the system.

Human-centered enterprise design supports save and resume, partial completion, reassignment, escalation, annotation, manual override, reopening, rollback, and rationale for exceptions.

Common ground and collective memory

Coordination depends on what people believe others know, which version they share, and whether responsibility is clear. A good B2B product reduces the amount of explicit communication needed to maintain that shared understanding.

Questions the product should answer

  • Who owns the next step?
  • What changed and why?
  • Has this been reviewed?
  • What happened while I was away?
  • Who understands this integration?

Useful mechanisms

  • Decision records and change rationale
  • Version and deployment state
  • Object-level comments
  • Handoff summaries
  • Expertise and ownership directories
Power matters. Visibility features can support coordination or become surveillance. Ask who sees the activity, what judgments will be made from it, whether the data is contextualized, and whether workers can challenge an incorrect interpretation.
Chapter 8

Automation, trust, control, and adoption

A system can be usable and still be poorly relied upon, rejected by the organization, or dangerous in practice.

Appropriate reliance

When the goal is neither maximum trust nor maximum skepticism
Academic grounding source

Lee and See frame trust as a factor that guides reliance when automation is complex and cannot be fully understood in every situation. Failures occur through misuse, including overreliance, and disuse, including underreliance.[11]

Industry application

Help users understand purpose, process, and performance: what the automation is for, how it works at a useful level, and how well it performs in relevant conditions. Show limitations, not just capabilities.

Measure

Measure correct acceptance and correct rejection of outputs. A trust-rating survey alone cannot tell you whether reliance was appropriate.

Mixed initiative and human control

When people and automation should contribute at different moments
Academic grounding

Mixed-initiative interaction treats control as something that can shift between human and system according to context, uncertainty, authority, and competence rather than defaulting to full automation or full manual control.

Industry application

Use approval thresholds, human escalation, preview, pause, cancel, correction, rollback, and compensating actions. Make the boundary between recommendation and execution unmistakable.

AI guideline bridge

Microsoft’s validated human-AI guidelines organize design needs around initial interaction, regular interaction, failure, and change over time.[12]

Technology acceptance

When adoption is shaped by the organization, not only the interface
Academic grounding source

UTAUT organizes technology acceptance around performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence, and facilitating conditions. Treat it as a model of adoption forces, not as a screen-design checklist.[13]

Industry application

Ask whether the tool visibly improves job performance, whether respected peers and managers support it, whether training and help exist, and whether integration, policy, permissions, and support make use practically possible.

Rollout implication

Onboarding may need role models, organizational legitimacy, and support. Continued use must prove performance value and fit the work.

Attention investment

When automation promises future payoff but demands effort and risk now
Academic grounding

Attention Investment models why users avoid abstraction and automation even when those tools could save time later. Programming demands an upfront specification cost, delays the payoff, and creates risk if the result is poorly understood.[6]

Industry application

Lower the first investment, show payoff early, make failure reversible, let users begin with a concrete example, and allow abstractions to emerge from repeated work rather than demanding them before value appears.

Agent implication

The adoption barrier may not be ignorance. It may be a rational response to the risk of specifying an autonomous system incorrectly.

Part III

Agent-building systems

Chapter 9

A builder tool is an end-user programming environment

Agent builders inherit decades of problems from programming tools, visual languages, workflow builders, and automation systems.

Calling the product “no-code” does not remove the cognitive work of programming. Users still decompose goals, choose components, coordinate dependencies, form hypotheses about behavior, debug failures, and manage abstraction.

Cognitive Dimensions of Notations

The Cognitive Dimensions framework evaluates the usability of representations and information artifacts rather than focusing only on surface interaction.[5]

DimensionAgent-builder questionPossible response
ViscosityHow much else must change when one data field or policy changes?Dependency-aware updates, refactoring support, reusable components.
Hidden dependenciesCan a builder see that a rule depends on a tool output or knowledge field?Dependency maps, references, impact analysis.
Premature commitmentMust users choose model, schema, permissions, or architecture before they understand behavior?Defaults, deferral, reversible choices, late binding.
Role expressivenessCan someone infer why each instruction, node, or tool exists?Intent labels, descriptions, grouping, examples, naming support.
Closeness of mappingDoes the representation match the business workflow or force technical translation?Domain concepts and vocabulary, multiple representations.
Progressive evaluationCan partial work be tested and inspected?Step runs, previews, test cases, sample data, replay.
Secondary notationCan layout, comments, grouping, and naming communicate rationale?Annotations, regions, visual grouping, documentation.
JuxtaposabilityCan users compare relevant states, versions, or components side by side?Diffs, split views, pinned references, compare mode.

The activity comes before the rating

No dimension is universally good or bad. A builder for exploratory design needs low viscosity, strong progressive evaluation, and little premature commitment. A tool for transcribing an already approved regulated workflow may prioritize precision, review, and constraint over free exploration.

Six learning barriers

Ko, Myers, and Aung identified six types of barriers in end-user programming: design, selection, coordination, use, understanding, and information.[7] The mapping to agent builders is direct:

BarrierAgent-builder manifestation
Design“I do not know how to decompose this workflow into agent behavior.”
Selection“Which model, tool, memory type, or component do I need?”
Coordination“How do instructions, tools, knowledge, and policies work together?”
Use“What exactly does this component do, and what are its constraints?”
Understanding“It ran, but the result is wrong. Why?”
Information“I have a hypothesis about the failure but cannot find evidence to confirm it.”
A common roadmap mistake Teams heavily invest in authoring and underinvest in understanding and information. For autonomous systems, debugging, traceability, testing, and observability are part of the core creation experience.

Part IV

Human-centered design with limited user access

Chapter 11

Designing responsibly when interviews are unavailable

No new primary research does not mean no evidence. It does mean your confidence should be lower and your assumptions more visible.

A PM-defined problem is a stakeholder hypothesis until supported by evidence. You can still design, but you should not present assumptions as established user needs. GOV.UK guidance recommends learning from users and continually validating needs, while explicitly planning around assumptions and research questions.[16]

An evidence ladder

Observation of actual workContext, interruptions, artifacts, coordination, exceptions.Observed cases may not represent all conditions.

Nielsen Norman Group’s guidance for complex applications reaches a similar practical conclusion: research the work and domain, combine perspectives across roles, adapt evaluation methods to expert reasoning, and use internal or adjacent experts pragmatically when direct recruitment is difficult, while acknowledging the limitation.[17]

The limited-access process

  1. Convert the brief into assumptions. Write what you believe about actors, context, knowledge, authority, frequency, risk, and desired outcome.
  2. Inventory existing evidence. Mine support tickets, call recordings, onboarding material, implementation notes, telemetry, policies, and current artifacts.
  3. Reconstruct the current work system. Map people, tools, data, handoffs, decisions, and exceptions.
  4. Use proxies intentionally. Speak with support, customer success, implementation, sales engineering, administrators, and recipients of the work. Label each source and bias.
  5. Design routine, complex, and exception scenarios. Do not evaluate only the happy path.
  6. Use theory as a risk detector. Ask what may be misunderstood, hidden, forgotten, overtrusted, or difficult to recover.
  7. Instrument the first release as research. Predefine predictions and collect evidence that can disconfirm them.

When you get one hour with one expert

Use the Critical Decision Method rather than a broad preference interview. It is an incident-centered cognitive task analysis method designed to elicit expert cues, expectations, decisions, and uncertainty.[8]

  1. Choose one difficult, nonroutine incident.
  2. Build a timeline of what happened.
  3. Revisit the incident and probe decision points.
  4. Ask what cues were noticed, what was expected, what alternatives were considered, and what a novice would miss.
  5. Ask how the outcome would change if key information or constraints were different.

This produces design-relevant knowledge about judgment and representation. It does not replace observation, but it is higher yield than asking, “Walk me through your typical workflow,” when access is scarce.

Chapter 12

Turn uncertainty into an assumption and measurement system

The honest alternative to invented certainty is explicit risk management.

Write assumptions so they can fail

Do not write “users need an easy agent builder.” Write:

  • Operations managers will build agents without engineering support.
  • They understand the workflow but not agent architecture.
  • The largest barrier is configuration, not organizational approval.
  • Visual workflow representation will help them predict behavior better than natural language alone.
  • The cost of a wrong external action is much higher than the cost of a wrong draft answer.

Prioritize by uncertainty and consequence

An assumption is urgent when it is both uncertain and capable of invalidating the product direction. This changes the conversation from “we need more research” to “three assumptions could sink this product, and these are the cheapest ways to test them.”

Chapter 14

Caveats and common mistakes

Theory improves reasoning only when its limits and evidence are visible.

Do not theory-wash

“We used cards because of chunking” is not a useful rationale. Explain what information must be considered together, why the grouping matches the work, and what behavior would show that it helped.

Do not begin with a list of UX laws

Fitts’s law, Gestalt principles, recognition over recall, and general heuristics can improve local interactions. They rarely determine the correct workflow, authority model, representation, exception policy, or division of labor between human and automation.

Do not confuse a business goal with a user need

A throughput target may align with user needs, or it may increase monitoring, pace, and burden. Ask who benefits, who absorbs the risk, and which quality dimensions may deteriorate.

Do not invent detailed personas from assumptions

Use role and context profiles. Mark what is known, inferred, and unknown. A fictional name and photograph do not increase evidence quality.

Do not use “cognitive load” to justify hiding everything

Complexity can belong to the domain. The task is to represent it so relationships, priorities, and consequences can be perceived, not to pretend it does not exist.

Do not mistake explanation for calibration

More explanation does not automatically improve reliance. People must be able to interpret the explanation and connect it to actual capability, uncertainty, and action.

Know the limits of the theories

  • Cognitive Work Analysis and ecological interface design have strong roots in safety-critical and process-control domains. Intentional business domains require adaptation.
  • Cognitive load research is largely rooted in learning and instructional settings. Use robust ideas such as element interactivity and expertise reversal cautiously rather than treating it as a universal interface formula.
  • UTAUT predicts acceptance forces and intention better than it prescribes interface design.
  • Proxy research systematically distorts. Support overrepresents breakdowns. Sales overrepresents buyers. Telemetry misses reasons and work outside the system.
  • Human-centered design must include affected people, power, fairness, privacy, accessibility, and organizational consequences, not only the efficiency of the primary operator.
A practical test for theoryIf citing a theory does not change a design decision, resolve a disagreement, expose a risk, or define an evaluation, it probably does not belong in the rationale.

Enterprise App Design Handbook

Cieden · Interface reference

Best UI referenceAtomic structureConcise and detailed answers

Cieden structures the handbook from small visual and interaction elements upward, following an Atomic Design inspired hierarchy. The published material currently focuses on the sub-atomic and atomic layers.

  • Atomic components: buttons, inputs, links, checkboxes, search, dropdowns, progress indicators, date pickers, accordions, and related accessibility decisions.
